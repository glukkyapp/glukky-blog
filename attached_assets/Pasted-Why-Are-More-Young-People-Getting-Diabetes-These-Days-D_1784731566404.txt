Why Are More Young People Getting Diabetes These Days?
Diabetes used to be thought of as something that only happened once you got older. But these days, even people in their thirties — sometimes even teenagers — are being diagnosed. So what's going on?

Are young people really more likely to get diabetes now?
Yes, and this trend is showing up all over the world. Diabetes used to mostly affect middle-aged and older adults, but now the number of teenagers and young adults being diagnosed keeps climbing year after year.

What's causing this?
The biggest factor has to do with body weight. Carrying extra weight, especially around the waist, makes it harder for the body to manage blood sugar properly. On top of that, today's lifestyle — eating out often, sitting for long hours, not moving enough, staying up late — puts extra strain on the body earlier in life.

Family history also plays a role. If someone in the family has had diabetes, younger relatives tend to carry a higher risk too.

Why does getting it young matter so much?
Being diagnosed young means living with diabetes for a much longer stretch of life. If it isn't managed well from the start, complications affecting the eyes or kidneys may show up earlier than expected. That's why catching it early and adjusting habits sooner really does make a difference down the road.

What can families do?
Keep an eye on weight and waist size, especially if diabetes runs in the family

Encourage young people to move more and sit less, building regular exercise into daily life

Cut back on sugary drinks and refined starches

If there's a family risk, regular blood sugar checks are the simplest way to stay ahead of it

This article is for general health information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. If a young person in your family is concerned about diabetes risk, please discuss testing options with a doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions

If a young person is thin, do they still need to worry about diabetes?
Weight is just one risk factor. Family history and lifestyle habits matter too, so even someone who's slim can still carry some risk.

Is diabetes in young people different from diabetes in older adults?
The underlying condition is similar, but since younger people will be living with it for many more years, getting it under control early matters even more.

Prediabetes Isn't a Done Deal — It Can Actually Be Turned Around
Hearing a doctor say "you have prediabetes" can feel heavy, like it's only a matter of time before it becomes full diabetes. But actually, prediabetes is a valuable stage — with the right steps, blood sugar can genuinely return to a normal range.

Does having prediabetes mean I already have diabetes?
No. Prediabetes means blood sugar is a bit higher than normal, but not yet at the level of diabetes. Think of it like a yellow light — a signal to pay attention, but there's still time to change direction.

Can it really be reversed?
Yes, it can. A large, long-running study in the United States found that adjusting diet and getting more exercise can significantly lower the chance of prediabetes turning into full diabetes — and this protective effect can last for many years. Other studies have also found that many people with prediabetes were able to bring their blood sugar back to normal simply by changing their daily habits.

What kind of changes actually help?
Try to eat meals at regular times and avoid overeating

Eat more vegetables, and cut back on refined starches and fried food

Take a walk or do some light exercise every day — even just ten or fifteen minutes helps

If you're carrying extra weight, losing even a little can have a real positive effect on blood sugar

Starting sooner rather than later tends to bring better results

Do I need to lose a lot of weight for it to work?
Not necessarily. Some research has found that even without a big drop in total body weight, improving daily habits and overall body condition can still help reverse blood sugar levels. So there's no need to put too much pressure on yourself — small, steady steps are enough.

This article is for general health information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. If you've been told you have prediabetes, please talk to your doctor about lifestyle changes that suit you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does prediabetes always turn into diabetes?
Not necessarily. Many people have successfully brought their blood sugar back to normal by changing their lifestyle.

Once it's reversed, am I completely in the clear?
Even after reversal, keeping up good habits matters to maintain the results, but the long-term protective effect is real.

A Few Things to Watch Out for This Summer If You Have Diabetes
Summer's here, and that means more outings — tea with friends, trips, walks around town. But when the weather heats up, blood sugar can start acting a little unpredictable. A few small habits can help you enjoy the season safely.

Why does hot weather affect blood sugar?
When it's hot, the body sweats more and loses water faster. If you don't drink enough to replace it, blood sugar can actually go up. On top of that, high heat can change how the body responds to medication or insulin, sometimes making low blood sugar more likely than usual.

What should I keep in mind when heading out?
Drink water often, even if you don't feel thirsty

Try to avoid going out during the hottest part of the day — mornings or evenings are more comfortable

While you're out, check in with how you're feeling — any dizziness, shakiness, or unusual tiredness

Wear light-colored, loose, breathable clothing, and bring a hat or umbrella for shade

Even at the beach or pool, wear sandals rather than going barefoot, so you don't accidentally hurt your feet without noticing

How should I store my medication and blood sugar monitor?
Insulin and certain medications don't like heat or direct sunlight, so never leave them in a car or somewhere they'll bake in the sun. When you're out and about, an insulated bag is a good idea — just make sure the medication doesn't touch ice directly. Blood sugar monitors and test strips are sensitive to heat too, so keep them somewhere cool whenever you can.

What if I start feeling unwell?
Feeling unusually tired, dizzy, having a racing heart, or clammy skin can all be signs that your body is warning you about heat exhaustion. If that happens, find some shade, sit down, and sip water slowly. If you don't feel better after resting, or if you start feeling confused, don't try to push through it — get help or see a doctor right away.

This article is for general health information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. If you feel unwell in hot weather, please seek help from family or a doctor as soon as possible.

Frequently Asked Questions

How should I carry insulin when I'm out for the day in summer?
An insulated bag works well — just keep it away from direct sunlight and don't let it touch ice directly.

Does sweating a lot mean something's wrong with my blood sugar?
Sweating alone doesn't necessarily mean there's a problem, but if it comes together with dizziness, a fast heartbeat, or extreme tiredness, it's worth paying closer attention — it could be a sign of heat exhaustion.
